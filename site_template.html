How it works

Where the projects come from

Every environmental review in Minnesota has to be announced in the state's weekly EQB Monitor. This site covers the 72 issues published June 2025 to November 17, 2026. Each issue is split into one entry per project. An AI reads every entry and sorts it:

  • Confirmed: the notice says data center.
  • Possible: it doesn't, but there are strong signs: "technology park," a code-named company, a huge building with no named user, or the same city as a known data center. A person needs to check these.
  • Power line: transmission tied to a data center or to "the customer's facility."

Plain code (not AI) then places each project at its city, township or county center and checks it against reservation, trust land and treaty cession boundaries.

Reading the map

Reservation

Land set aside for a tribal nation by treaty or federal law, governed by the tribe.

Off-reservation trust land

Land outside a reservation held in trust by the U.S. for a tribe or tribal members.

Cession area where rights were reserved

Land Ojibwe bands ceded in the 1837, 1842 and 1854 treaties while reserving rights to hunt, fish and, under some of these treaties, gather. Courts and agreements with the state have recognized those rights.

Other treaty cession area

Land ceded to the U.S. by treaty, such as the 1851 Dakota treaties. What rights were kept varies by treaty.

Limits

  • Cession boundaries come from 1890s maps (Royce) digitized by the U.S. Forest Service. They're approximate, and they are not a determination of anyone's rights or interests.
  • Treaty rights and tribal interests are not limited to the lines shown. Being inside or near a boundary doesn't establish legal impact, and a proposal on treaty land doesn't by itself violate treaty rights.
  • Project locations are city, township or county centers, not parcels. Distances are approximate.
  • None of the projects tracked so far sits on reservation or trust land. Almost all of Minnesota lies inside some treaty cession area, so the more telling measures are distance to a reservation and whether a project is in an area with reserved off-reservation rights.
  • "Possible" projects are unverified. Some notices likely describe the same site, so 13 projects is about 9 places.
  • Public data only. No cultural, sacred or harvest sites, and no tribal data.

About

Why this exists

Data centers are being proposed across Minnesota, some within a few miles of reservations and inside areas where tribes reserved treaty rights. Cities and counties usually run the environmental review and aren't required to consult tribes. State agencies that must consult often come in later. The notices are public but easy to miss: many never say "data center," and comment windows are often 30 days.

Data Center Watch for Tribal Nations tries to close that gap with public information, so tribal staff don't have to wait for a developer or a local government to tell them a project is coming.

Who it's for

Tribal environmental and natural resource staff first. Community members, advocates and reporters too.
