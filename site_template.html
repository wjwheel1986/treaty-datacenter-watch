Treaty-Territory Data Center Watch

Know what's being proposed on treaty land, and when you can still speak up.

Photo: Lake Superior, in the 1854 treaty area. Courtney Celley, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (public domain).

What's happening

  • Data centers are being proposed all over Minnesota, fast. They can use a lot of water and electricity, and many need new power lines.
  • Some sit on land Ojibwe bands ceded in the 1854 treaty, where they kept the right to hunt and fish. Others are on land Dakota nations ceded in 1851.
  • A city or county usually runs the environmental review. They don't have to consult tribes. The notices are public, but they're buried in a weekly state bulletin, and many never use the words "data center." Look for "technology park" or a company called "Project Something, LLC."
  • Comment windows are short. Often 30 days.

What this site helps you do

  • Find out early. An AI reads every weekly state notice and pulls out the data center projects, including the ones hiding behind other names.
  • See where they are. Each project sits on the map next to reservations and treaty land, with the distance to the nearest reservation.
  • Know what you can still do. Review stage, whether comments are open, who makes the decision, and links to the official notices. In plain language.

Built for tribal environmental and natural resource staff first. Useful to anyone keeping an eye on these projects: community members, advocates, reporters. It's an early warning, not an authority. What the colors and shapes mean

People use "tribal land" to mean different things. The map shows three kinds of boundary, plus the projects.

Reservation

Land set aside for a tribal nation by treaty or federal law. The tribe governs it. Shown solid green, with the name when you zoom in.

Off-reservation trust land

Land outside a reservation that the U.S. holds in trust for a tribe or tribal members. Shown lighter green.

Treaty area where rights were kept

Land Ojibwe bands ceded in the 1837, 1842 and 1854 treaties while keeping rights to hunt, fish and, under some of these treaties, gather. Courts and agreements with the state have recognized those rights. The tribe doesn't own this land, but the rights still apply. Brown, solid outline.

Other treaty cession area

Land a tribal nation ceded to the U.S. by treaty, like the 1851 Dakota treaties. What rights, if any, were kept varies by treaty. Light brown, dashed outline.

No shading

No tribal boundary on this map. That doesn't mean no tribal interest. Many nations have ties to places outside these lines.
